Transferability
The EFC Tools provide a standardised approach, but applying them in a new jurisdiction requires tailoring to context rather than direct replication:
Adapt the assessment methodology to context.
 Where subnational climate, fiscal and governance data are limited, lean on interviews and policy review — as the India pilot did with its desk review and seven stakeholder interviews — while reflecting the real intergovernmental relationships in play where mandates are unclear, and calibrating the access-to-finance analysis to the actual depth of local financial systems and intermediation.
Deploy the National and Subnational Tools together,
 so a new jurisdiction is assessed as a coherent multilevel system rather than at a single level in isolation, with the national baseline framing the subnational diagnosis, as in the India and Indonesia pilots.
Secure access to stakeholders and source documentation
 for the data-collection, inception and validation steps — the policies, budgets and officials the process depends on — much as the India pilot used its two New Delhi roundtables to confirm findings.
Ground the assessment in government ownership and existing planning and budget cycles,
 so its recommendations are taken up as live reforms; in India, the gaps mapped directly onto instruments already in use, such as Central Finance Commission grants and NMSH 2.0, rather than requiring new mechanisms.
Identify 
technical partners
 
to run the workshops, interpret the scored dimensions and translate findings into roadmaps, as GIZ did alongside MoHUA, NIUA and TERI in the India pilot.
Enabling conditions
Availability of governance, financial and climate-related data,
 since the depth of accessible data bounds what any assessment can conclude, and strengthening subnational climate data is itself one of the four EFC dimensions.
